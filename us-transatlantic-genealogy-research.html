Research for US clients and cross-border families

US & Transatlantic
Genealogy Research

Professional research for families whose records cross the United States, Britain, Canada and Europe, carried out remotely from Cumbria.

Home/Services/US and transatlantic genealogy research

Some family lines cross several record systems before the trail becomes clear. A person may change the spelling of a name after migration, pass through Canada on the way to the United States, or leave descendants whose DNA points back to a British or European family that is difficult to recognise in the records.

Based in Cumbria, working remotely with US clients: I combine digitised American records, online archives, direct enquiries to record custodians, UK archive research and DNA evidence where each is relevant to the question.

Cross-border questions I can investigate

  • Where in Britain or Europe did a US immigrant originate?
  • How did a family move between US states, Canada and overseas locations?
  • Why did a surname, given name, birthplace or reported age change?
  • Can DNA matches connect an American branch to a documented ancestral family?
  • Can a Black or mixed-race family line be traced through difficult pre-1870 evidence?
  • Does an existing US lineage remain reliable when the original records are checked?

Following the family through different record systems

Depending on the place and period, research may use US federal and state censuses, vital and church records, newspapers, city directories, immigration and passenger lists, naturalisation files, probate, land, military and cemetery sources. Overseas work may continue into British civil and parish records, European civil registration, local church material, archive catalogues and direct correspondence with record custodians.

Where DNA is relevant, I use it to group descendants and test possible links between branches. Record coverage varies widely by state, country and period, so the first task is to identify which jurisdiction and source is most likely to answer the particular question.

How I follow a family across borders

  1. Define the question. We identify the person, relationship or migration problem the first stage should address.
  2. Review the starting evidence. I check the records, trees, DNA information and earlier research already available.
  3. Research across jurisdictions. I follow the family through the relevant US, Canadian, British or European record systems.
  4. Correlate the result. Dates, places, occupations, relatives, associates and DNA evidence are compared before a conclusion is stated.

Before I start tracing the migration

  • Known names, dates, locations and relationships
  • Copies or links for certificates, census records and family documents
  • An existing tree, GEDCOM or view-only tree link if available
  • Immigration clues, family stories and known overseas places
  • DNA platform and match information when genetic evidence is relevant

Scope and timing

A focused record or identity question may fit the Quick Search. Research that crosses several states, countries or generations is charged at £35 per hour and normally begins with a five-hour stage, so the evidence can be reviewed before deciding what to pursue next.

The estimated timetable depends on record access, archive or church responses, the number of jurisdictions and whether DNA match trees must be reconstructed. Prices on this site are shown in GBP, and the scope and cost are agreed before work begins.

How the results are returned

The agreed output may include a written report, source citations, record images or references, a research log, a family tree or relationship chart and recommendations for unresolved questions. Where the evidence is incomplete, I distinguish firm findings, probable conclusions and open hypotheses.

What cross-border research can establish

The result depends heavily on how precisely an immigrant can be identified on both sides of the journey. Sometimes the records reveal the exact place of origin; sometimes they narrow the search to one family or district. If two candidates still fit, I leave that question open and explain what might distinguish them.
