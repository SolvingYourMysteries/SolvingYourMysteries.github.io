Cumberland genealogy case study

Proving a Family Connection
to John Peel of Caldbeck

A family story travelled from Cumberland to Australia and eventually to a descendant in Austria. John Peel’s name had survived for generations, but nobody knew exactly how the family was connected to him.

Home/Case Studies/John Peel family connection

By Brittany Warren · Published 24 September 2026

Privacy note: This case study is published in anonymised form. The client’s name and identifying modern details have been omitted, while the broad migration history has been retained. Deceased relatives are named where they are needed to explain the research.

A client asked me to investigate her father’s family from Cumbria. Among the stories passed down through the family was a supposed connection to John Peel, the nineteenth-century Cumberland huntsman associated with Caldbeck.

Several recent generations were already known, which gave me a useful place to begin. The family had moved from Cumberland to Australia, and the client now lives in Austria. Despite those moves, John Peel’s name had remained part of the family story.

The difficulty was that nobody knew where he belonged in the family tree.

Starting with John Peel

I began by researching Peel’s immediate family separately from the client’s ancestry.

Cumbrian Lives identifies John Peel (1776–1854) as the Caldbeck-area huntsman later celebrated in D’ye Ken John Peel? and names his parents as William Peel and Lettice Scott. The Historic England listing for John Peel Cottage also records Peel’s association with Greenrigg in Caldbeck parish.

I checked records relating to his baptism, marriage and children so that I had a clear picture of his family before looking for a connection with the client.

This soon showed that the client did not descend from John Peel himself. The family story, however, had never preserved a precise relationship. It was still possible that the link lay elsewhere in Peel’s family.

Following the two families

With Peel’s immediate family established, I returned to the client’s ancestry and continued working backwards from the relatives she already knew.

Her paternal line led through Cartmell and Jackson families and, several generations earlier, to a woman named Nancy Scott.

That surname was immediately interesting because Peel’s mother was Lettice Scott. Both women were also associated with the same part of Cumberland.

A shared surname and locality can suggest a line of enquiry, but they cannot establish a relationship on their own. I therefore looked for records that identified the members of the Scott family and showed how they were connected.

Parish baptisms, marriages and burials were compared with probate material and wills. Names, dates, places and family relationships were checked across the surviving records.

The evidence showed that Nancy Scott and Lettice Scott were sisters.

That was the point at which the two branches joined.

The client descended from Nancy Scott. Lettice Scott married William Peel and became the mother of John Peel. John Peel was therefore the son of the client’s seventh-great-aunt.

Checking the connection against original records

Connections involving well-known people need particular care. Public family trees are useful for finding possible leads, although an incorrect relationship can easily be copied from one tree into many others.

For this case I used biographical material to establish the outline of Peel’s known family, then checked the relationships through genealogical records.

Parish and church records supplied baptisms, marriages and burials. Wills and probate material helped identify relatives and distinguish between people with similar names. Civil registration, census material and later family records were used as the client’s ancestry moved into more recent generations.

The important part was the continuity of the evidence. Each generation needed to connect with the next, including the Scott generation where the client’s ancestry met Peel’s maternal family.

John Peel’s grave at Caldbeck

John Peel is buried at St Kentigern’s Church in Caldbeck.

His memorial records members of his immediate family and provides useful evidence for dates and family context. It is also a tangible link with a man whose name has remained familiar in Cumberland long after his death.

The client’s relationship to him could not be established from the memorial itself. That emerged from the records concerning the Scott family, particularly the evidence identifying Nancy and Lettice as sisters.

How the family story survived

What I found was more interesting than a simple famous-ancestor claim.

At some point in the generations between Cumberland and the present day, the precise relationship had disappeared from memory. John Peel’s name remained.

That is easy to understand. Few families preserve terms such as “son of a seventh-great-aunt” in oral tradition. A remembered relationship is much more likely to survive in a shortened form.

In this case there was enough substance in the old story to point towards a genuine family connection. The records supplied the part that memory had lost.

Further work on the family

The first stage of research took five hours and answered the original question about John Peel. It also identified several branches that could be explored in more depth.

The Scott family can be taken further through parish, probate, land and local records. The client’s Cartmell ancestors have long roots in Cumberland and Westmorland, where archive material may reveal more about property, occupations and the communities in which they lived.

The move to Australia is another substantial piece of the family’s history. Passenger lists, civil registration, probate records, electoral rolls and newspapers may help establish when particular relatives left Britain, where they settled and what happened to them after arrival.

There is also further archival material concerning John Peel himself. A Cumbria Archive Service catalogue update lists local-history material relating to Peel and the song, including an article titled John Peel, Yeoman and Hunter from Caldbeck and an 1897 piece about the history of D’ye Ken John Peel?

Those sources could add something different to the pedigree: a clearer picture of Peel’s place in the local community and of the way his reputation developed after his lifetime.

For researchers with Cumberland or Westmorland ancestry, my guide to Cumbria Archive Centres explains the kinds of records available locally and how archive research can extend what is found on the major genealogy websites.

A family story with an answer

This project began with a piece of family lore that had crossed continents and survived for generations.

Researching the client’s ancestry and John Peel’s family separately eventually brought both lines to the same Scott family in Cumberland. Nancy Scott, the client’s ancestor, was the sister of Lettice Scott, John Peel’s mother.

The relationship was more distant than the family story may once have suggested, but the name had been remembered for a reason.

For me, that is one of the most interesting parts of researching family stories. Even when some of the detail has disappeared, an old name, place or relationship can preserve a clue worth investigating.

Have a family story you would like to investigate?

If your family has long associated itself with a particular person, place or historical family, it helps to begin with the generations closest to the present and gather whatever records, certificates, photographs and family information are already available.

From there, I can assess what survives in parish registers, wills, civil records, newspapers, archives and other sources, and suggest a research scope suited to the question.
