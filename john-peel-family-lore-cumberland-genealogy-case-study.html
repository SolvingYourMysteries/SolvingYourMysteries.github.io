Cumberland genealogy case study

Proving a Family Connection
to John Peel of Caldbeck

A family story had travelled from Cumberland to Australia and on to a descendant in Austria. The question was simple; proving the answer required two family trees to meet in the middle.

Home/Case Studies/John Peel family connection

By Brittany Warren · Published 24 September 2026

Privacy note: this case study is published in anonymised form. The client’s name and identifying modern details have been omitted, while broad migration context is retained. Deceased historical relatives are named only where they are necessary to explain how the relationship was proved.

A client came to me wanting to know more about her father’s family from Cumbria. One story had been repeated for years: somewhere in the family there was supposed to be a connection to John Peel, the famous Cumberland huntsman associated with Caldbeck. The family had never been able to prove it.

The question: was the family story actually true?

The client already had a useful starting point, with several recent generations identified. Her wider family had moved from Cumberland to Australia, while she now lives in Austria, so the story had survived a remarkable amount of geographic distance as well as time.

What she did not have was a documented path from her known family to John Peel. That distinction matters. A story can be valuable evidence about what a family believed, but it is not proof of a biological relationship. My job was to treat the story as a research clue and test it against records.

First, I researched John Peel independently

Before trying to connect the client to a famous name, I built and checked John Peel’s immediate family for myself. That meant comparing biographical material with parish and family records rather than simply copying an existing online tree.

Cumbrian Lives identifies John Peel (1776–1854) as the Caldbeck-area huntsman later celebrated in D’ye Ken John Peel?, and names his parents as William Peel and Lettice Scott. The Historic England listing for John Peel Cottage also connects Peel closely with Greenrigg in Caldbeck parish.

I checked his baptism, marriage and children so that I understood the documented Peel family before looking for any point of contact. Quite quickly, I could rule out a direct line of descent from John Peel.

That did not disprove the family story, because the story was about being related to him rather than necessarily descending from him. It narrowed the research question: if the connection was real, where did the two wider families meet?

The key shift: from direct descent to a collateral relationship

Family stories often shorten a complicated relationship into something much simpler. “We are descended from John Peel” and “we are related to John Peel” can become interchangeable after several generations, even though genealogically they mean very different things.

Instead of forcing the client’s line into Peel’s descendants, I worked in two directions. I traced the client’s documented family backwards, one generation at a time. Separately, I traced John Peel’s ancestry back through his parents and then followed the wider Scott family through siblings and their descendants.

This is sometimes the most effective way to test a famous-ancestor story: do not begin by trying to make the connection fit. Build each side independently until the records either meet or they do not.

Where the two trees met: the Scott sisters

The breakthrough came on the maternal side of John Peel’s family. His mother was Lettice Scott. On the client’s own documented line, I had reached Nancy Scott, from the same part of Cumberland.

The shared surname and locality were useful clues, but neither was enough. Scott was not so unusual that two women should be linked simply because they lived in the same region. I needed evidence that Nancy and Lettice belonged to the same family.

I therefore correlated parish baptisms, marriages and burials with probate evidence and wills, checking names, dates, places and family relationships across the records. Those sources established Nancy Scott and Lettice Scott as sisters. Peel’s own records then connected Lettice to her son John.

That gave the family story a documented structure: John Peel was not the client’s direct ancestor, but he was the son of her seventh-great-aunt.

Why this is stronger than finding the same names in an online tree

Connections to famous or locally well-known people are especially vulnerable to copying. Once one public family tree attaches a notable person to a line, the same claim can spread through dozens of trees without anyone re-checking the original records.

For this case, I did not treat a biography, an online tree or the family story itself as the final answer. Each had a different purpose:

  • Family lore supplied the research question.
  • Biographical sources helped identify John Peel’s known family and gave leads to verify.
  • Parish and church records established baptisms, marriages and burials.
  • Wills and probate material helped distinguish relatives and support family relationships.
  • Later civil, census and family records carried the client’s line forward through subsequent generations.

The relationship only became a conclusion when the chain on both sides could be supported. If one generation had depended solely on somebody else’s unsourced tree, the famous connection would still have been a possibility rather than a proved family relationship.

What the grave adds — and what it does not

John Peel is buried at St Kentigern’s Church in Caldbeck. His memorial is an evocative piece of family history and records several members of his immediate household. It is valuable evidence for dates and family context, but the grave alone cannot establish how a modern descendant connects to him. That required the less visible work of reconstructing the Scott family and proving the sibling relationship.

The value of the family story

The result is a good example of why I do not dismiss family lore simply because the wording turns out to be imprecise. The story was not correct if it had been understood to mean direct descent from John Peel. But its central claim — that the family was related to him — was true.

Somewhere across generations of migration and retelling, the exact relationship had been lost while the important name survived. The family story had preserved a real connection, but documentary research was needed to recover its shape.

Research principle: family stories are leads, not conclusions. The aim is neither to believe them automatically nor to disprove them for the sake of it, but to identify what evidence would confirm, refine or contradict the story.

What else could be researched?

The initial five-hour project answered the main question, but it also opened up several avenues for further research. The client’s other family lines have deep roots in Cumberland, and there is plenty of scope to explore them in greater depth through parish records, wills, land and property records, newspapers and material held in local archives. This could help move beyond names and dates to build a fuller picture of where the family lived, how they made a living and the communities they were part of.

The Australian branch offers another distinct research story. Passenger lists, state civil registrations, probate records, electoral rolls and newspapers could help establish when different relatives left Britain, where they settled and how the family developed after migration. It would also be interesting to investigate whether stories about the family’s Cumberland origins — including the connection to John Peel — continued to be passed down after the move to Australia.

There is also a considerable amount more that could be explored about John Peel himself. A search of the Cumbria Archive Service catalogue reveals a wide range of material relating to Peel, his image and the way his story was remembered long after his death. This includes British and Australian newspaper articles about John Peel and John Woodcock Graves, the writer associated with D'ye Ken John Peel; typescripts about Peel and Cumbrian fox-hunting; photographs and postcards of his grave, cottage and birthplace; and even genealogical papers for other Cumbrian families with a recorded connection to John Peel. There are also records relating to the later history and popularity of the song itself.

That amount of surviving material means there is scope to take the research much further than simply proving a relationship. A future archive visit could investigate Peel as a real person within his local community, the history of the places associated with him, how his reputation developed after his death and how connections to him were recorded by other Cumbrian families. As with any archive catalogue search, though, each item would need to be assessed carefully: John Peel was not a unique name, and a catalogue result alone is never proof that a record belongs to the famous Caldbeck huntsman.

For anyone researching families from this part of the country, my guide to Cumbria Archive Centres explains how local archive research can take a family tree beyond the records available on the major genealogy websites. If you have a family story of your own that has never quite been proven, this is exactly the sort of problem I can investigate — starting with what can be documented and following the evidence wherever it leads.

What this case demonstrates

  • Start with the known family and work backwards. A famous name should never become the starting assumption.
  • Research the target independently. Understanding John Peel’s parents and wider family was essential before any connection could be tested.
  • Collateral lines matter. The answer was not in Peel’s descendants but in his mother’s sister.
  • Two trees can meet in the middle. Building the client’s line and the target family separately reduces the risk of forcing a desired result.
  • Proof is cumulative. Shared surnames and places are clues; a supported relationship depends on records that connect one generation to the next.
  • A family story can be wrong in detail but right in substance. In this case, the surviving lore had preserved a genuine relationship for generations.

Have a family story you would like to test?

If your family has always said that you descend from — or are related to — a particular person, place or historical family, the most useful first step is to gather the recent generations you can already document and identify exactly what the story claims.

I can then assess whether the connection is testable through lineage verification, local archives, parish registers, wills, newspapers or wider family-network research. You are welcome to send me the story and what you already know, and I can suggest a sensible scope without assuming the answer in advance.
