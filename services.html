Adoption & Unknown Parentage

Parentage and adoption research can involve living relatives, sensitive discoveries and incomplete records. I approach these cases privately and build the family connection from the available DNA and documentary evidence.

The work may involve clustering DNA matches, reconstructing the families of matches and checking ages, places and records until the possible relationships can be narrowed down.

I build carefully structured cases using:

  • DNA clustering techniques
  • Family tree reconstruction of matches
  • Timeline and geographic comparison
  • Civil registration and corroborating documentation
  • Indirect evidence and elimination methodology

Genetic Genealogy

DNA can help with many questions besides recent adoption, especially where the paper trail becomes uncertain several generations back.

I analyse shared matches and family networks, then compare the genetic picture with documented trees. That combination can confirm a branch, challenge an assumed relationship or point towards a family that the records alone did not identify.

DNA analysis can help to:

  • Break through brick walls
  • Confirm or challenge existing research
  • Identify shared ancestors
  • Reconstruct extended family networks
  • Support documentary findings with genetic evidence

03

Document-Based Research

Most projects begin with documentary research. I locate the records most relevant to the question and compare them across time, place and family relationships.

Depending on the case, that may mean civil certificates, parish registers, wills, newspapers, military records, migration sources or local archive collections. Findings are returned with source references so you can see where each piece of information came from.

Records I work with include:

  • Civil registrations (birth, marriage, death)
  • Parish and church records
  • Probate records and wills
  • Census and electoral rolls
  • Military, land, and court records
  • Newspaper archives
  • Immigration and passenger lists
  • Local archival collections

04

Brick Wall & Complex Cases

For a stubborn family-history problem, I begin by checking the evidence already collected and identifying where the uncertainty actually starts.

The next useful record may belong to a sibling, neighbour, witness or entirely different surname. I use timelines, family networks, migration patterns and alternative record sets to test the possibilities one by one.

My approach includes:

  • Reconstructing entire family networks to identify one individual
  • Analysing migration patterns
  • Identifying naming traditions and kinship structures
  • Evaluating conflicting or incomplete records
  • Developing and testing multiple research hypotheses

05

US, Transatlantic & International Research

For American clients and families whose records cross national borders, I connect evidence from the United States with records in Britain, Canada, Europe and other relevant regions.

A family crossing borders may change its spelling, reported birthplace or even the records in which it can be found. I follow the family through the relevant jurisdictions and use DNA when it can help connect branches on opposite sides of the Atlantic. Other regions can be assessed case by case according to language, access and record survival.

Research may include:

  • US federal and state census records
  • Vital, church, cemetery and newspaper sources
  • Immigration, passenger and naturalisation records
  • Canadian and cross-border family research
  • British and European civil, parish and archive records
  • DNA evidence connecting overseas branches

Historical Context & Narrative Reporting

Some projects benefit from more than a pedigree. A report can also explain the places, occupations and events that shaped the family being researched.

I use relevant local and social history to explain what the records mean in context, without losing sight of the evidence specific to your ancestors.

Reports may include:

  • Social and economic background
  • Occupational research
  • Geographic and community context
  • Historical events influencing family movement
  • Clearly written research summaries with full citations

Family Tree Building

I can build a new family tree from a known person or review an existing tree that has grown difficult to verify.

Each relationship is checked against records, with sources added and uncertain connections marked rather than carried forward as fact.

If you are researching for yourself and are not sure where to begin, start with my beginner's guide to starting a family tree.

I assist with:

  • Building family trees using reliable, verifiable records
  • Reviewing existing trees for errors, inconsistencies, or unsupported connections
  • Organising and restructuring complex or disordered research
  • Adding clear source citations and documentation
  • Identifying gaps and recommending next steps for further research

08

Lineage Verification

Lineage verification is a focused check of an existing line of descent. I work through it generation by generation, locating the records that support each parent-child relationship and identifying any weak or conflicting links.

The finished work shows which links are well supported, where the evidence is incomplete and what would be needed to resolve any remaining gap.

For an example, see the John Peel family-connection case study, where a long-standing family story was tested by building both sides of the relationship independently and proving the collateral link through records.

I verify lineage using:

  • Civil registration records and parish registers
  • Census and household records
  • Probate, land, and legal documents
  • Correlation of multiple independent sources
  • Resolution of conflicting evidence
  • Clear documentation and source citation

How I work

How I approach a research question

I start with the question you want answered and the evidence already available. From there I choose the record sets, people and places most likely to move the case forward, keeping notes of what I search and why.

Some projects end with a firm identification; others narrow the possibilities or show where the records run out. I explain those limits plainly, and sensitive findings are handled privately.
