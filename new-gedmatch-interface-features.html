What Changed in the New GEDmatch Interface

GEDmatch's redesigned interface is now live, bringing easier navigation, better match organisation and a much more practical research workflow. These are the features I think matter most for genealogists.

Home/Guides/New GEDmatch Interface

By Brittany Warren · Published 25 August 2026

GEDmatch is useful when you want to compare DNA results outside the company where a test was taken, though the old interface could be hard to learn. The redesigned site reorganises the same core comparison work around a cleaner set of menus, search tools and project-management features.

The DNA comparisons themselves will feel familiar to existing users. The bigger change is the route between them: finding a tool, carrying a group of kits into another analysis and keeping notes or folders attached to an active project now takes fewer steps.

The changes I would notice first

1My Clipboard for carrying matches between tools
2A redesigned One-to-Many match list
3A searchable catalogue of GEDmatch tools
4DNA kits and family-tree data in one place
5Folders, column controls, search and sorting
6One combined One-to-One comparison
7Optional user profiles
1

My Clipboard makes repeated kit work easier

My Clipboard is the feature I expect to use most often in day-to-day DNA research.

Multi-Kit Analysis often involves the same set of kit numbers across several comparisons. My Clipboard lets you collect those kits once and carry the group into other tools, cutting out repeated copying and pasting.

This is especially useful when several matches appear to descend from the same ancestral couple. You can keep the relevant kits together, move them into another comparison and continue the analysis without rebuilding the group each time.

Why I like it: it turns GEDmatch from a collection of separate tools into something closer to a connected research workspace.
2

The redesigned One-to-Many makes new matches easier to spot

The One-to-Many DNA Comparison is where many GEDmatch investigations begin. It compares one kit against other kits in the database and produces a match list showing people who share DNA with you.

The redesigned One-to-Many adds new-match highlighting and time-based sorting. When you return to a kit after several weeks or months, those controls make it much easier to see what has appeared since the last review.

Recent matches can therefore be reviewed as new evidence instead of disappearing into an old list. In unknown-parentage and brick-wall cases, one new tester can sometimes connect a cluster that previously made little sense.

Best use: revisit important kits regularly and concentrate first on genuinely new matches rather than repeatedly reviewing the same list.
3

A searchable tool catalogue replaces menu memorising

GEDmatch has accumulated a large set of tools over the years, and beginners often know the task they want to perform before they know the name of the GEDmatch tool that performs it.

The redesign adds a searchable catalogue with categories and descriptions. You can look for matching, segment analysis, clustering or tree work directly, without learning the old dashboard first.

I think this is particularly important for people who upload their DNA to GEDmatch because someone has told them they should, but then have no idea what to do next. Better descriptions should reduce the gap between uploading a kit and actually using it to answer a genealogy question.

For beginners You do not need to learn every GEDmatch tool at once.

Start with the question you are trying to answer. For most people, One-to-Many, One-to-One and a small number of organisational tools are a much better starting point than opening every application on the site.

4

DNA kits, GEDCOMs and FamilySearch imports are easier to manage together

The new interface centralises the management of DNA kits, GEDCOM family trees and FamilySearch tree imports. That may not sound as exciting as a new matching tool, but good data management is what makes a large DNA project sustainable.

If you manage several tests — your own, parents, grandparents or other relatives — it is easy for a dashboard to become cluttered. Bringing the associated data into a clearer management area should make it easier to see what belongs to whom and which kits have trees or other supporting information attached.

A match establishes shared DNA. Family trees, records, locations and relationships are what usually reveal why that DNA is shared, so keeping tree information close to the kits is genuinely useful.

5

Folders, column controls and site-wide sorting should make big projects manageable

GEDmatch says the new design uses folders, column controls, search and sorting as a site-wide standard. This is one of those changes that becomes more valuable as a project becomes more complicated.

A researcher dealing with an unknown grandparent, adoption case or difficult paternal line may have dozens of matches under consideration. The ability to organise information consistently is essential if you want to distinguish promising evidence from people you have already investigated and ruled out.

Folders can also encourage researchers to organise by research question rather than trying to solve everything at once: for example, “maternal grandfather”, “Kentucky Logan line”, “unknown great-grandmother”, or “chromosome 7 cluster”.

Research tip: use folders and notes to record conclusions as well as possibilities. Knowing why you ruled a match out can be just as useful six months later as remembering why you saved one.
6

Autosomal and X-DNA comparisons now sit together

The new interface includes a combined One-to-One tool that can toggle between autosomal DNA and X-DNA rather than requiring researchers to treat them as separate destinations.

One-to-One comparisons are useful after you have identified a particular match and want to inspect the shared DNA in greater detail. Bringing the two comparison types together should make the workflow more intuitive, particularly for researchers who do not use X-DNA frequently enough to remember where the separate tool is located.

X-DNA has its own inheritance pattern and should not be interpreted in the same way as ordinary autosomal DNA, but when it is relevant it can help eliminate some ancestral lines from consideration. Making the option easier to reach is therefore a useful interface improvement without changing the underlying genetic analysis.

7

Optional profiles add context to a kit number

GEDmatch is also introducing optional user profiles. This is a different kind of improvement: it does not change the DNA comparison itself, but it could make communication between researchers more useful.

A kit number on its own tells you very little about the research interests of the person who manages it. A profile could potentially give useful context about surnames, locations or genealogy interests and make it easier for two people working on the same family to recognise that connection.

Because profiles are optional, researchers can decide how much they want to share. As with any genealogy platform, I would avoid publishing information about living relatives or anything you would not be comfortable making visible to other users.

A likely workflow for a real research case

Taken together, the changes make it easier to move through a research question in a sensible order.

Step 1Run One-to-Many
Step 2Save promising matches
Step 3Compare kits in detail
Step 4Organise the evidence

I would start with One-to-Many and identify the matches most likely to answer the question. I would then add the relevant kits to My Clipboard, run the appropriate One-to-One or multi-kit comparisons, and use folders, notes and sorting to keep the evidence organised.

The sequence becomes straightforward: find a useful match, investigate it, compare the relevant group and record what you learned before moving on.

What has not changed A better interface does not replace genealogical evidence.

Shared DNA can suggest a relationship, but identifying the correct ancestor still requires family trees, records, locations, ages, relationships and careful testing of alternative explanations.

Which changes are worth learning first?

For existing users, the useful changes are mostly practical: finding the right tool, spotting a recent match, carrying a group of kits between analyses and keeping a project organised. Those improvements do not change the underlying DNA, but they make an active case easier to manage.

That is especially useful for people who have previously opened GEDmatch, seen a long page of tools and closed it again. The platform has always offered far more than a basic match list. A more understandable interface could make those tools accessible to many more family historians.

Existing GEDmatch users are likely to notice My Clipboard and the redesigned One-to-Many first. New users may get more immediate value from the searchable tool catalogue and the clearer kit-management area.
