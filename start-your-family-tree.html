A simple place to begin

You do not need to know the right words

Family history can quickly feel full of unfamiliar websites, records and terminology. You do not need to understand any of that before getting in touch.

Tell me what you know in ordinary language — even if it is only a name, an approximate place or a story passed down in the family. I search reliable records, compare the evidence and explain what I find clearly. We can begin with one small question rather than committing to a large project.

What would you like to do?

Choose a comfortable starting point

Most first projects fit one of these straightforward options. You do not have to decide which one is right before contacting me.

01

Begin a family tree

Start with yourself, a parent or a grandparent and work backwards using records. The first stage can focus on one side of the family, one couple or one generation.

For example: “I know my grandparents’ names. Can you help me discover who came before them?”

02

Find one person or record

Look for a birth, marriage, death, census or parish record, or investigate where one relative lived or came from.

For example: “Can you find the marriage of my great-grandparents in Cumberland?”

03

Check what you already have

Review an online tree, certificate, handwritten note or family story and work out what is supported, what is uncertain and what to try next.

For example: “An online hint gives me another generation, but how do I know it is the right family?”

What happens next

A clear first step, with no pressure

  1. Tell me what you knowSend the names, approximate dates, places, photographs, certificates or family stories you already have.
  2. I suggest a sensible starting pointI review the information and explain what could be researched first. You do not need to choose a technical service yourself.
  3. You receive a quoteI set out the proposed question, likely research time and cost. You decide whether you would like to continue.
  4. I research and explain the resultYou receive clear findings, source details and practical next steps. Where the evidence is uncertain, I say so.

You do not need

Anything perfectly organised

  • An Ancestry or other paid subscription
  • A DNA test
  • Exact dates or perfect name spellings
  • A large, organised family tree
  • To know which archive or record set to search
  • To commit to a complete family history

It is completely normal for some details to be guesses. Just tell me which parts you are unsure about.

Preparing your enquiry

What should you send?

Send whatever you already have. A useful starting enquiry usually includes:

  • The name of the person or family you want to explore
  • Any approximate dates, even if you are not certain
  • The village, town, county, state or country connected with them
  • Copies or photographs of certificates, letters or notes
  • A link to an existing online tree, if you have one
  • The one thing you would most like to discover

A simple message you can use

“I would like to learn more about [name], who lived in [place] around [date]. I already know [what you know]. The main thing I would like to find out is [your question].”

Starting small

Common first questions

A few things people often wonder about

I only know a name. Is that enough?

It may be enough to begin a conversation. A rough date, place, relative or occupation can make the search much more focused, but you should still send the information you have rather than waiting until it feels complete.

Do I need to take a DNA test?

No. Most starter family trees and record searches begin with documents such as certificates, census returns and parish registers. DNA is useful for some questions, but it is not a requirement.

Can I do some research myself?

Yes. You can ask me to investigate one problem, check a connection or suggest the next records to look for, then continue the tree yourself. Professional research does not have to replace your own interest in the family.

Do you only research families from Cumbria?

No. I am based in Cumbria and have particular experience with Cumberland, Westmorland and local archives, but I also research families elsewhere in the UK, the United States and other countries.
