Online newspaper research guide

Newspapers.com vs
British Newspaper Archive

Both can uncover family notices, local events and unexpected detail. The useful question is which collection covers the right place, title and date.

Home/Guides/Newspapers.com vs British Newspaper Archive

By Brittany Warren · Last checked: 30 September 2026

For a family rooted in Britain or Ireland, I would normally begin with the British Newspaper Archive. For a family that crossed the Atlantic or moved through several countries, Newspapers.com may become just as important. Neither site replaces the other, and a search of one is not a search of both.

The point to remember: the collections overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The British Newspaper Archive holds niche UK titles that Newspapers.com does not. A fruitless Newspapers.com search is therefore no reason to skip the BNA.

Coverage: British depth or international reach?

The British Newspaper Archive is a partnership between Findmypast and the British Library. Its centre of gravity is British and Irish publishing, including national papers, county and town newspapers, and publications written for particular trades, faiths, political movements or communities. That local and specialist material can matter more to a genealogist than a famous national title.

Newspapers.com has a broader international shape. Its official updates regularly describe material added through partnerships in several countries. This makes it particularly useful when a British family settled overseas, when a traveller or serviceman was reported abroad, or when a story was reprinted far from the place where it began.

Looking at British Newspaper Archive vs Newspapers.com as a simple contest misses the practical issue. Before paying for either service, check whether it carries the newspaper, edition and date range that could answer your question. A title may be present for some years and absent for others. A town may also have been covered by a paper published in a neighbouring district.

Regional and niche UK titles

This is where the BNA often earns its place in British family-history research. It includes local and special-interest newspapers that never became household names, and some are not available on Newspapers.com. Those less familiar titles may contain the useful material: a village report, court appearance, chapel anniversary, trade notice, inquest, school event or letter from a serviceman.

Do not search only by the modern county or the nearest large town. Newspapers reported across district boundaries, changed names and produced local editions. Search the catalogue by place, then inspect each title's available years. If your ancestor lived close to a county border, include papers published on both sides.

The reverse check still matters. Newspapers.com may have a useful run, edition or overseas report that is absent from the BNA. Record which titles and years you have checked so that “I searched newspapers” does not become an unrepeatable note.

Wildcards for surname variants

Both sites support ? and * wildcards, which are useful when a spelling varies or OCR has misread part of a word. On Newspapers.com, ? replaces one letter and * replaces multiple letters. The BNA uses the same basic idea in its Keywords box: one ? stands for one unknown character, ?? for two, and * for an unknown number of characters. BNA wildcards can appear at the start, middle or end of a term and can be used more than once.

Worked surname examples:

Armstr?ng can cover a one-letter uncertainty such as Armstrong/Armstrang.

Johnst*n can catch several forms where the middle of the surname varies.

On the BNA, put wildcard searches in the Keywords box. On either site, begin fairly broadly and then narrow by place, title or date.

Exact phrases and BNA proximity searching

On Newspapers.com, quotation marks keep two or more words together. Search "Margaret Irving" when you want the first name and surname next to each other; if that is too restrictive, remove the quotation marks and narrow by location or year instead.

The BNA also supports exact phrases, but its Advanced Search adds a particularly useful extra: you can specify how many words may appear between the terms in a phrase. This is effectively a proximity, or “nearby”, search.

BNA proximity example:

"Hyde Park demonstration" ~0 requires the terms to sit next to one another.

"Hyde Park demonstration" ~1 allows up to one word between the terms.

"Hyde Park demonstration" ~2 allows up to two words between them.

For genealogy, the same technique can loosen a phrase just enough to catch OCR or wording differences without opening the search as widely as three completely separate keywords.

Boolean operators, exclusions and multiple names

Newspapers.com supports OR and NOT. OR is useful for alternatives; NOT removes a repeated false match.

"John Scott" OR "Jno Scott" looks for either form of the forename.

"John Scott" NOT football can reduce results about a sportsman when your ancestor had no connection with football.

The BNA handles exclusions differently. In the Keywords box, put a minus sign before a word you do not want. For example, Victory -HMS -road -street can help when you are looking for a pub called the Victory rather than ships or addresses. Its Names box also accepts several names separated by commas, which can be useful for a wedding, funeral or court report involving more than one known person.

When terms need to appear near each other

The BNA's ~ phrase-flexibility control is the more precise tool here because you can decide how much separation to allow. Newspapers.com supports quotation marks for words that must stay together, but not a numerical proximity operator.

Whichever site you use, read the newspaper image rather than relying only on the highlighted OCR snippet. A large page may contain several unrelated columns, and two matching terms can belong to different stories.

Fuzzy searching and variant spellings

The BNA says its newer search is more forgiving of wording and sentence structure. That is helpful for topics, but it should not be treated as a promise that every surname variation will be found automatically. Search the forms your ancestor might actually have used: a nickname, initials, a married name, an occupational title or a common misspelling.

Newspapers.com gives more visible control through wildcards and OR. For a surname found as Armstrong and Armstrang, try Armstr?ng, then search each spelling separately if the results remain thin. Also try the name of a spouse, parent, child or business partner; OCR may have failed on one name and read another correctly.

Date, place and newspaper-title filters

Filters are most useful after a broad search has shown how the database describes the material. On the BNA, you can narrow by date, newspaper title and publication place, sort by publication date or recent addition, and browse issues of a chosen title. Advanced Search can also limit results to front pages.

Newspapers.com allows date and location filtering, narrowing to a newspaper, and searching within a title or issue. Its additional filters can include particular result types and the date material was added. Thumbnail previews help you judge whether several hits come from one page before opening each result.

A useful order of work: search a name without a title filter; note which papers appear; narrow to the most relevant place and decade; then search within the likely paper. Starting with a very tight filter can hide an article printed just outside your expected district.

OCR quality: why a known article may not appear

Both services use optical character recognition, or OCR, to turn a newspaper image into searchable text. The search engine is reading that machine-made transcription rather than understanding the page as a person would.

Old type, narrow columns, broken letters, creases, stains, faint microfilm and unusual page layouts can all produce errors. A surname may be split at the end of a line, a capital letter may be mistaken for another character, or a word may disappear from the searchable text altogether. The scan can still be perfectly readable to you.

When a search fails, try a shorter name, a spouse or child, the street, farm, occupation, ship, regiment, court or event. Remove quotation marks. Search a nearby date. If you know the likely paper and day, browse the issue page by page. A negative keyword search is evidence about the OCR and the collection, not proof that the event went unreported.

Pricing and access options

The BNA gives you several ways to pay for access without committing everyone to the same plan. Some material is marked free to view. For a small, defined piece of research there is pay-per-view access, while regular users can choose monthly, three-month or annual subscriptions. The practical choice depends on how many articles you expect to open and how long the project is likely to last.

Newspapers.com divides subscription access into Basic and Publisher Extra. Basic covers the material included at that level; other titles or years are marked as Publisher Extra and require the higher access level. Before subscribing, search for the newspaper and years you actually need so you know which tier covers the material relevant to your family.

It is also worth checking your local library, archive, university or family-history society, as institutional access may be available and the rules for using it from home vary by organisation.

Clippings, saving and downloading

Newspapers.com lets you draw a clipping around an article, give it a title, add tags and return to it from your Clippings area. You can then print the clipping, download it as a JPG or PDF, share it with others without them needing a Newspapers.com subscription, and save it to an Ancestry tree. Keep the newspaper title, date and page with any downloaded image so the finding can be cited later.

On the BNA, the image viewer's Clip and save tool lets you keep either the full newspaper page or a selected area. Once the clipping is made, you can download a high-resolution copy, print it, save it to a collection or Workspace, copy it to the clipboard, or share it. Collections and Workspaces are useful when a project involves several people, places or newspaper searches that you want to keep together.

The BNA also allows personal sharing of discoveries, including sharing a clipping directly or copying a link to send by email or social media.

Whichever site you use, save more than a cropped sentence. Keep enough of the page to understand the article, the masthead or title, publication date, page number and edition. Transcribe names and places exactly as printed, then add your interpretation separately.

Which should you use for Cumbrian ancestors?

For an ancestor who remained in Cumberland, Westmorland or the area now called Cumbria, I would begin with the British Newspaper Archive. Its local and regional coverage includes titles that are simply not available on Newspapers.com. Two useful Carlisle examples are the Carlisle Journal and the Carlisle Patriot: both are on the BNA, but not on Newspapers.com. For families in and around Carlisle, that difference alone can materially change what a search turns up.

Start with the place as it was known at the time. A present-day Cumbrian village may have been catalogued under Cumberland, Westmorland or Lancashire North of the Sands. Search the nearest market town as well as the parish, and include neighbouring counties when the family lived close to a border. My guide to tracing ancestors in Cumberland and Cumbria explains why those historic jurisdictions matter.

Then check Newspapers.com rather than assuming the work is finished. It may hold different years, editions or reprints, and it becomes especially useful when a Cumbrian family emigrated. An Australian, Canadian or American newspaper may report the death of a migrant, mention relatives still in Britain or repeat a story first printed locally.

Online newspaper sites do not cover every surviving issue. If the title or year you need is missing, consult the catalogue and local-studies information for the relevant repository. The guide to Cumbria Archive Centres is a useful next step, while my Cumbria genealogy research service covers focused archive and local-record work.

How to use both archives together

  1. Define the question. Write down the person, event, place and likely date range. A search for “everything about this surname” is hard to evaluate.
  2. Check coverage before searching. Look for the likely title, neighbouring titles, editions and years on each site. Note the gaps.
  3. Begin with the BNA for a British or Irish locality. Put known people in Names and a place, occupation or event in Keywords. Then try an exact phrase, a ~1 or ~2 proximity search, and exclusions where they help.
  4. Repeat the search with variants. Use ? and * in the BNA Keywords box, then try initials, nicknames, married names, relatives and old place names. Remove tight filters if nothing appears.
  5. Move to Newspapers.com. Re-run the core search, then use quotation marks, ?, *, OR and NOT where they solve a specific problem.
  6. Browse when OCR is the obstacle. If the date and paper are known, inspect the issue manually. Search a relative or address to find the right page.
  7. Save the evidence and the search history. Record site, title, date, edition, page, search terms and access date. Keep a clipping or image with enough context to identify the source.

This combined method also makes a negative result more useful. You can state which collections, titles, years, name variants and browsing steps were checked, rather than simply recording that an online search found nothing.

Newspaper archive questions family historians ask

Which is better for UK family history: Newspapers.com or the British Newspaper Archive?

For research centred on Britain or Ireland, the British Newspaper Archive is usually the first collection to check because it is built around British and Irish newspapers and includes many regional, local and specialist titles. Newspapers.com is still worth checking because the collections are not identical and it is particularly useful when a family moved overseas or appeared in international coverage.

Do Newspapers.com and the British Newspaper Archive have the same newspapers?

No. The collections overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The British Newspaper Archive holds niche UK titles that Newspapers.com does not. Always check the title, edition and years available on each site before treating a search as complete.

Is Newspapers.com useful for British ancestors?

Yes. Newspapers.com carries UK material and can be especially useful for British families who migrated, travelled, served abroad or were mentioned in overseas newspapers. Its wildcard and Boolean search tools are also useful when names were spelt inconsistently.

Why can I not find a newspaper article that I know exists?

The relevant title or year may be absent, the article may sit in a different edition, or OCR may have misread the name. Try spelling variants, relatives' names, an address, occupation or place, then browse the likely issue if the date is known.

Should I subscribe to both newspaper archives?

Not necessarily. Check which titles and years cover your research question first. The BNA offers free-to-view material, pay-per-view and several subscription lengths, while Newspapers.com separates access into Basic and Publisher Extra. Using both becomes worthwhile when a family moved between countries, when local coverage is patchy, or when one site's OCR misses an article found on the other.

Can I search historical newspapers without paying?

The British Newspaper Archive identifies a selection of free-to-view pages and also offers pay-per-view alongside subscriptions. Library or institutional access may also be available locally. Newspapers.com access and trial terms can change, so check its current plan page before beginning a project.

If your newspaper search has reached a dead end

Newspaper research can be surprisingly time-consuming, especially when names are common, spellings vary or the OCR is poor. Sometimes the useful article is there, but finding it means trying different search terms, checking neighbouring titles or following the family through other records first.
